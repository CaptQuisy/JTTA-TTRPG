## Social Encounters <!-- {docsify-ignore} -->

Social encounters occur whenever characters attempt to influence another person through conversation, negotiation, intimidation, deception, leadership, performance, or other forms of personal interaction.

A social encounter may be as simple as convincing a guard to allow passage or as involved as negotiating a treaty between hostile factions.

Social encounters are an important part of _Journey Through the Ages_. Characters may gain valuable information, secure assistance, avoid dangerous conflicts, establish alliances, or accomplish their objectives without resorting to violence.

Most ordinary conversations do not require dice rolls. When the outcome is uncertain and failure would have meaningful consequences, the Game Master may call for a **Social Skill Check**.

Social Skill Checks should resolve uncertain reactions, not replace roleplaying. What the character says, what they want, and how they approach the situation determine the appropriate Skill, Difficulty, and modifiers.

### Social Skill Checks

Social encounters use the standard Skill Check rules.

The player rolls an appropriate Attribute + Skill dice pool and counts each 5 or 6 as one success.

The Game Master determines the number of successes required, either by assigning a Difficulty or using an opponent's Social Defense.

#### Common Social Approaches

|Approach|Possible Check|
|---|---|
|Persuade or negotiate|Presence + Influence|
|Intimidate through personality|Presence + Influence|
|Intimidate through physical threat|Strength + Influence|
|Detect a lie or hidden motive|Acuity + Investigation|
|Entertain or impress|Presence + Performance|
|Present a logical argument|Intelligence + Influence|
|Impersonate someone|Presence + Performance|
|Question or interrogate|Presence + Investigation|
|Distract someone|Presence + Performance|

These combinations are guidelines.

Different approaches may justify different Attribute and Skill combinations.

For example, an investigator might use Intelligence + Investigation to identify contradictions in a suspect's statement, while another character might use Acuity + Perception to notice nervous behavior.

A character's approach should determine the check, not simply whichever Attribute or Skill provides the largest dice pool.

#### Resolving a Social Encounter

When a Social Skill Check is necessary, follow these steps:

1. **Establish the Goal:** The player describes what the character wants the target to do, reveal, or believe.
2. **Describe the Approach:** The player explains how the character attempts to achieve that goal.
3. **Choose the Check:** The Game Master selects an appropriate Attribute and Skill.
4. **Determine Resistance:** Use the appropriate Social Difficulty or the target's Social Defense.
5. **Apply Modifiers:** Consider the environment, circumstances, relationship, leverage, and target's Personality Traits.
6. **Roll the Dice:** The player rolls the dice pool and counts successes.
7. **Resolve the Outcome:** Compare successes with the required number and describe the target's response.

The Game Master should establish the intended outcome before the roll.

A successful check should produce the reasonable result the player was attempting to achieve.

### Social Difficulty

The target's attitude and the nature of the request establish the starting Difficulty.

|Difficulty|Successes|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Easy|1|Asking a friendly person for a minor favor|
|Normal|2|Convincing a neutral person to cooperate|
|Hard|3|Asking someone to accept meaningful inconvenience or risk|
|Extreme|4|Convincing a hostile person to cooperate|
|Legendary|5|Asking someone to betray a deeply held belief, duty, or important ally|

These examples represent general guidelines.

The Game Master should consider the actual request rather than relying solely on the target's attitude. 

A friendly bartender may readily provide gossip but refuse to hide a wanted murderer. Likewise, a hostile opponent may temporarily cooperate when doing so clearly serves their interests.

#### Impossible Requests

Some requests cannot succeed through ordinary social influence. No amount of charm will normally convince a loyal guard to murder their commander without extraordinary circumstances or leverage. Similarly, a character cannot persuade someone to reveal information they do not possess.

When a request is impossible under the circumstances, the Game Master should explain why the attempted approach cannot accomplish the desired result.

The characters may need to discover new information, establish leverage, change the situation, or pursue a different objective.

#### Social Influence Is Not Mind Control

A successful Social Skill Check does not remove a target's judgment, personality, loyalties, or freedom to make decisions.

Success means the character achieves a plausible result within the circumstances established for the check.

Persuasion may convince someone to cooperate.

Deception may cause someone to believe a false statement.

Intimidation may compel temporary compliance through fear.

None of these approaches automatically creates permanent loyalty or unconditional obedience.

### Social Interactions Against Opponents

Important opponents may have a **Social Defense** rating.

Social Defense represents confidence, self-control, experience, authority, and resistance to persuasion, deception, intimidation, and manipulation.

When a character directly attempts to influence an opponent with a defined Social Defense, use that rating as the number of successes required instead of assigning a normal Social Difficulty.

The Game Master does not roll dice for the opponent.

#### Example: Negotiating with a Crime Boss

A Crime Boss has **Social Defense 4**.

A character attempts to persuade the Crime Boss to release a prisoner.

The Game Master calls for a Presence + Influence Check.

The character must roll at least 4 successes to succeed.

Appropriate modifiers may apply based on the character's leverage, the Crime Boss's motivations, and the circumstances of the negotiation.

### Choosing Difficulty or Social Defense

Use the following guidelines:

**Ordinary Social Difficulty:** Use when the target does not have a defined Social Defense or when the challenge is better represented by the general difficulty of the request.

**Social Defense:** Use when a character directly attempts to influence an important opponent with an established rating.

Do not combine the ordinary Social Difficulty and Social Defense into a single target number. The required successes come from one or the other.

If the request is impossible under the circumstances, the existence of a Social Defense rating does not make it possible.

### Social Modifiers

After determining the required successes, consider circumstances that make the character's approach more or less effective.

These circumstances generally modify the character's dice pool rather than changing the number of required successes.

#### General Modifier Guidelines

|Circumstance|Modifier|
|---|---|
|Minor advantage|+1 die|
|Major advantage|+2 dice|
|Overwhelming advantage|+3 dice|
|Minor disadvantage|–1 die|
|Major disadvantage|–2 dice|
|Overwhelming disadvantage|–3 dice|

Modifiers from several distinct sources may apply.

However, the Game Master should avoid excessive stacking. Usually, the two or three most important circumstances are enough.

A circumstance should not be counted twice simply because it appears in more than one modifier category.

For example, if a hostile target's attitude is already represented by an Extreme Difficulty, do not automatically impose the additional –2 dice hostility modifier for that same attitude.

Apply the penalty only if there is a distinct reason for it.

### More Detailed Modifiers (Optional)

For a more detailed set of social encounter modifiers, there are options for adjusting for Environment, Situation, and Personality.  

#### Environment

The surroundings can significantly affect a conversation.

|Circumstance|Modifier|
|---|---|
|Comfortable, private, relaxed setting|+1 die|
|Target is on familiar or friendly ground|–1 die|
|Character has arranged an impressive or authoritative setting|+1 die|
|Loud, crowded, or distracting environment|–1 die|
|Poor communication because of distance or noise|–1 die|
|Dangerous or chaotic surroundings|–1 die|
|Target feels physically threatened by the location|+1 die to Intimidation|
|Character is obviously out of place|–1 die|
|Character has privacy for a sensitive conversation|+1 die|
|Conversation is being watched by people the target fears|–1 to –2 dice|

Environmental modifiers should reflect how the surroundings affect the specific approach.

A dark interrogation room might help an Intimidation attempt while making an effort to establish friendship or trust more difficult.

A private meeting may help a character discuss sensitive information, but privacy offers little benefit when the target has no reason to fear being overheard.

##### Example: A Private Conversation

A character wants to persuade an official to reveal confidential information.

The official fears being overheard.

The character arranges a private meeting in a secure location.

The Game Master grants +1 die because privacy directly addresses the official's concern.

The character does not receive multiple bonuses merely because the location is both private and quiet.

#### Situation

The circumstances surrounding the request are often more important than the character's personal charisma.

|Circumstance|Modifier|
|---|---|
|Request clearly benefits the target|+1 die|
|Character offers a valuable reward|+1 to +2 dice|
|Character possesses useful leverage|+1 to +2 dice|
|Character provides convincing evidence|+1 to +2 dice|
|Target urgently needs the character's help|+2 dice|
|Request costs the target time or effort|–1 die|
|Request involves significant personal risk|–2 dice|
|Request threatens target's livelihood or reputation|–2 dice|
|Request could result in imprisonment or death|–3 dice|
|Character has fulfilled a promise to target|+1 die|
|Character previously betrayed or deceived target|–2 dice|
|Target knows character is lying|–3 dice or automatic failure|
|Character has credible authority over target|+1 to +2 dice|
|Character lacks expected credentials or status|–1 to –2 dice|
|Target is already suspicious|–1 die|
|Target is hostile|–2 dice|
|Target owes character a significant favor|+2 dice|

These modifiers represent circumstances that materially affect the target's willingness to cooperate.

They should not be applied automatically without considering the request.

##### Leverage and Incentives

Leverage may include information, rewards, threats, obligations, or opportunities.

The strength of the modifier depends on how meaningful the leverage is to the target.

A large financial reward may strongly influence a greedy merchant but have little effect on an official who values personal honor above money.

Likewise, a credible threat may influence someone who fears personal harm while provoking resistance from someone determined to protect their family.

##### Example: Offering a Reward

A character attempts to convince a ship captain to transport the party through dangerous waters.

The captain is reluctant because the journey presents significant risks.

The character offers a substantial payment.

The Game Master grants +2 dice because the reward provides a meaningful incentive.

The risk of the journey remains relevant and may impose a separate penalty.

#### Personality

NPC personalities should influence how social encounters unfold.

Important NPCs may possess one or more **Personality Traits** representing their motivations, values, ambitions, and weaknesses.

Characters who discover and exploit these traits may receive bonuses to appropriate Social Skill Checks.

##### Personality Traits

|Personality|Effective Approach|Modifier|
|---|---|---|
|Greedy|Money, valuables, profit|+1 die|
|Proud|Flattery, recognition, appeals to reputation|+1 die|
|Honorable|Duty, promises, fairness|+1 die|
|Compassionate|Protecting or helping others|+1 die|
|Patriotic|Appeals to country or community|+1 die|
|Religious|Appeals consistent with beliefs|+1 die|
|Ambitious|Advancement, status, opportunity|+1 die|
|Cowardly|Credible threats or demonstrations of danger|+1 die|
|Suspicious|Evidence and verifiable facts|+1 die|
|Curious|Secrets, mysteries, unusual information|+1 die|
|Loyal|Protecting friends, family, organization|+1 die|
|Rebellious|Defying authority or convention|+1 die|

An approach that directly conflicts with a Personality Trait may instead impose –1 or –2 dice.

For example, attempting to bribe an exceptionally honorable officer may make negotiations more difficult rather than easier.

A character must know or reasonably infer the trait before deliberately exploiting it.

Personality Traits should guide NPC behavior rather than reduce complex characters to predictable reactions.

An honorable official may still accept a reward for legitimate assistance. A greedy merchant may still refuse a deal that threatens their family.

##### Assessing Motivations

Characters may attempt to discover another person's motives, concerns, loyalties, or intentions before making a social appeal.

This may involve:

- Careful observation.
- Conversation.
- Investigating the target's background.
- Examining inconsistencies in statements.
- Learning about relationships and obligations.
- Identifying fears, ambitions, or personal interests.

The Game Master may call for an appropriate Skill Check when the information is uncertain.

Common approaches include:

**Acuity + Investigation:** Recognizing hidden motives or contradictions.

**Intelligence + Investigation:** Researching the target's history or circumstances.

**Acuity + Perception:** Noticing meaningful behavior or changes in expression.

**Presence + Influence:** Drawing out information through conversation.

The appropriate check depends on the method used.

A successful assessment may reveal a Personality Trait, useful leverage, or another fact that can influence later interactions.

##### Example: Discovering a Guard's Motivation

A character learns that a city guard is worried about his family's safety.

Instead of attempting to bribe the guard, the character offers to help the family escape the city.

The Game Master may grant +1 or +2 dice to the subsequent Influence Check because the character has discovered meaningful leverage.

The bonus comes from the character's knowledge and offer, not merely from the player's eloquence.

### Roleplaying and Good Arguments

Social Skill Checks should support roleplaying without requiring players to possess the same social abilities as their characters.

A shy player should be able to portray a charismatic diplomat.

A player who struggles with improvising dialogue should still be able to play a skilled negotiator. The player may describe the character's intended argument rather than delivering it word for word.

For example:

_"I explain that the governor will benefit politically if he supports our proposal."_

This is sufficient to establish the character's approach.

Players should not receive bonuses merely for being personally charismatic or entertaining at the table. Instead, reward meaningful preparation, discovered information, relevant leverage, and creative approaches.

Likewise, eloquent roleplaying should not automatically overcome an impossible request. The character's abilities and the established circumstances remain important.

### Degrees of Success

Social encounters can produce more than simple success or failure.

The Game Master may use the optional degrees-of-success rules when a variable outcome would improve the encounter.

| Result               | Effect                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Failure              | Target refuses, becomes suspicious, demands something in return, or creates another appropriate complication                                           |
| Success              | Target agrees to a reasonable request or provides the desired information                                                                              |
| Additional Successes | Target may provide additional information, improve their attitude, reduce their price, offer assistance, or cooperate beyond the original request      |
| Critical Failure     | Target becomes hostile, recognizes deception, ends negotiations, raises an alarm, spreads damaging information, or otherwise makes the situation worse |

#### Critical Failure

In a social encounter, it may create a significant complication.

For example:

- A guard realizes the character is lying.
- A merchant becomes offended and ends negotiations.
- A suspect alerts an accomplice.
- A noble publicly insults the character.
- A hostile faction becomes aware of the character's intentions.

The consequence should be appropriate to the attempted action.

### Extended Social Encounters

Important social situations may require several meaningful exchanges rather than a single Skill Check.

Examples include:

- Negotiating a treaty.
- Conducting a complex interrogation.
- Arguing a case during a trial.
- Persuading a hostile council.
- Building support among rival factions.
- Negotiating with a powerful crime boss.
- Attempting to win over a suspicious group.

An **Extended Social Encounter** represents a developing conversation in which characters gather information, make arguments, address objections, and respond to changing circumstances.

#### Establishing the Encounter

Before the encounter begins, the Game Master determines:

1. The characters' objective.
2. The important participants and their motivations.
3. The number of accumulated successes required.
4. The maximum number of failed checks permitted.
5. The consequences of success or failure.

A typical extended encounter might require:

**5 accumulated successes before 3 failures.**

The Game Master may adjust these values according to the importance and complexity of the encounter.

#### Different Approaches

Characters may use different Skills and Attributes during an extended encounter, provided their actions meaningfully advance the objective.

For example:

- Influence may establish rapport.
- Investigation may uncover motivations.
- Performance may impress an audience.
- Academics may support an argument with historical or legal knowledge.
- Perception may identify reactions that reveal which arguments are effective.

The Game Master should require a clear connection between the action and the encounter's objective.

Repeatedly making the same argument without new information or a changed situation should not automatically generate another Skill Check.

#### Consequences of Success

When the characters accumulate the required successes, they achieve the established objective.

The Game Master may also describe additional benefits justified by their approach and any exceptional results.

#### Consequences of Failure

When the characters reach the failure limit, the encounter ends without achieving its established objective.

Possible consequences include:

- Negotiations breaking down.
- The target refusing further discussion.
- An opportunity being lost.
- The target demanding more favorable terms.
- The characters attracting suspicion.
- A rival faction gaining an advantage.

Failure should follow naturally from the developing encounter.

The Game Master should avoid introducing severe consequences that were not reasonably foreseeable unless a Critical Failure or another established circumstance justifies them.

#### Attempting Again

Characters cannot normally repeat an extended encounter immediately with the same arguments and circumstances.

A new attempt requires a meaningful change.

Examples include:

- Discovering new evidence.
- Obtaining additional leverage.
- Offering different terms.
- Changing the target's circumstances.
- Establishing a new relationship.
- Waiting until an appropriate opportunity arises.

The Game Master determines whether another attempt is possible.

### Game Master Guidance

Social encounters should reward preparation, meaningful choices, and attention to NPC motivations.

The Game Master should use Skill Checks to resolve uncertainty without replacing character interaction.

**Make Motivations Matter**

- Important NPCs should possess understandable interests, loyalties, fears, and ambitions.
- Characters who discover these motivations should gain meaningful advantages.

**Reward Information, Not Player Eloquence**

- Bonuses should come from relevant leverage, preparation, and circumstances rather than a player's personal speaking ability.

**Avoid Excessive Modifiers**

- Apply only the modifiers that meaningfully affect the encounter.
- Do not count the same circumstance more than once.

**Respect Established Character Motivations**

- A successful Social Skill Check should not force a target to act in a way that is impossible or fundamentally inconsistent with the established circumstances.

**Keep Failure Interesting**

- Failure may introduce new complications, reveal what the target wants, or require a different approach.
- It should not always bring the adventure to a halt.

**Use Extended Encounters Selectively**

- A brief conversation may require no roll or a single Skill Check.
- Reserve extended encounters for important negotiations and social conflicts where multiple meaningful decisions can influence the outcome.

**The goal of Social Encounters is to make conversation, negotiation, deception, and diplomacy as engaging and consequential as physical challenges, while preserving the importance of character abilities and player choices.**
